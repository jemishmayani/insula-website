Chiari malformation

In a Chiari malformation, the lowest part of the cerebellum, called the tonsils, extends down through the opening at the base of the skull into the top of the spinal canal. Many people have no symptoms, but some need surgery to make more room.

What it is

The opening at the base of the skull, the foramen magnum, is where the brainstem joins the spinal cord. Normally the cerebellar tonsils sit just above it. In Chiari malformation type 1, they extend below it, usually because the back of the skull is a little small.

The tonsils can crowd the opening and interrupt the flow of cerebrospinal fluid. In some people, fluid collects inside the spinal cord, forming a cavity called a syrinx.

Treatment

Normal

The lowest part of the cerebellum, the tonsils, sits just above the opening at the base of the skull, the foramen magnum. Fluid flows freely up and down around the brainstem and spinal cord with each heartbeat.

Low-lying tonsils

The tonsils sit lower, into the opening. A small descent is common on scans and often causes no symptoms.

Chiari malformation

The tonsils plug the opening like a cork. Fluid flow is blocked, causing headaches at the back of the head that are typically worse with coughing or straining. Fluid can be forced into the spinal cord, forming a cavity called a syrinx, which can cause numbness and weakness in the hands.

After decompression

A small piece of bone at the back of the skull, and often the back of the first neck bone, is removed, and a patch widens the lining. This makes room for the tonsils, restores fluid flow, and often lets the syrinx shrink.

Symptoms

  • Headache at the back of the head or neck, typically brought on by coughing, sneezing, laughing or straining
  • Dizziness, unsteadiness or poor coordination
  • Difficulty swallowing, or a hoarse voice
  • With a syrinx: numbness, tingling or weakness in the hands and arms, often with loss of temperature feeling, and sometimes a curving spine (scoliosis)

Many Chiari malformations are found by chance on scans and never cause symptoms.

How it is diagnosed

An MRI scan of the brain and spine shows how low the tonsils sit and whether there is a syrinx. Special MRI sequences can show the fluid flow.

Treatment options

  • Monitoring. If there are no symptoms and no syrinx, surgery is usually not needed.
  • Posterior fossa decompression. A small piece of bone is removed from the back of the skull, often with the back of the first neck bone, and a patch is usually sewn into the lining to widen it. This makes room, restores fluid flow and often lets a syrinx shrink.

Surgery is mainly aimed at stopping symptoms worsening. Headaches brought on by coughing often improve well; numbness from a long-standing syrinx may improve less.

When to seek urgent help

  • Difficulty swallowing or breathing
  • Rapidly worsening weakness or numbness in the arms or legs
  • Severe headache with vomiting and drowsiness

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.
