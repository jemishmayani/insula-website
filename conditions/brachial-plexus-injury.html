    Nerves

    Brachial plexus injury

    The brachial plexus is the network of nerves that runs from the neck, behind the collarbone and into the armpit, and controls the whole arm. When it is stretched, torn or pulled from the spinal cord, part or all of the arm can become weak, numb and painful. Some injuries recover on their own. Others need surgery, and timing matters.

    What it is

    Five nerve roots, called C5, C6, C7, C8 and T1, leave the spinal cord between the bones of the lower neck and upper chest. They join, split and regroup in a set pattern, and end as the main nerves of the arm. Together they move the shoulder, elbow, wrist and hand, and carry feeling from the skin.

    Because each root supplies particular muscles, the pattern of weakness shows which roots are injured. Choose a pattern below to see which nerves are affected and which movements stop working.

    A healthy plexus

    Five nerve roots leave the spinal cord in the lower neck and upper chest. They join, split and regroup behind the collarbone, then run into the armpit as the nerves that move and feel the whole arm. Choose a pattern to see what an injury affects.

    Upper injury (C5–C6), also called Erb's palsy

    The most common pattern, in adults and in babies. The shoulder can't lift and the elbow can't bend, so the arm hangs by the side, turned inwards. The hand still works. The outer shoulder and arm may be numb.

    Upper and middle injury (C5–C7)

    As for an upper injury, and the wrist and fingers also drop because the muscles that lift them are weak. Grip is usually still present.

    Lower injury (C8–T1), also called Klumpke's palsy

    Less common. The shoulder and elbow work, but grip and fine hand movements are weak, and the hand may curl into a claw. A droopy eyelid and small pupil on the same side (Horner's syndrome) suggest the T1 root has been pulled from the spinal cord.

    Whole plexus injury (C5–T1)

    The whole arm is weak or paralysed and numb, and it often hurts. This usually follows a high-energy injury such as a motorcycle crash, and some roots may have been pulled out of the spinal cord.

    Causes

    • Road accidents, especially motorcycle crashes, are the most common cause in adults. The head and shoulder are forced apart, stretching or tearing the nerves.
    • Falls onto the shoulder, and sports injuries.
    • Cuts and gunshot wounds near the neck or armpit.
    • Birth. The nerves can be stretched during a difficult delivery, for example when a baby's shoulder gets stuck. This affects about 1 to 2 in every 1,000 births.
    • Less often, a tumour pressing on the nerves, radiotherapy to the area, or inflammation of the nerves.

    Types of injury

    How badly the nerve is damaged decides whether it can recover by itself.

    • Stretch (neurapraxia). The nerve stops working for a while but is not torn. It usually recovers over weeks to months.
    • Rupture. The nerve is torn outside the spine. It won't heal by itself, but it can often be repaired.
    • Avulsion. The root is pulled out of the spinal cord. It can't be reattached, but other nerves can be rerouted to restore some movement.

    Many people have a mix of these in different roots.

    Three ways a nerve root can be injured

    Type of injury

    A healthy nerve root

    Each root leaves the spinal cord as tiny rootlets, passes its ganglion (a small swelling of nerve cells) in the gap between two vertebrae, and runs on to the arm.

    Stretch (neurapraxia)

    The nerve is stretched but not torn. Signals stop for a while. Most stretch injuries recover by themselves over weeks to months.

    Rupture

    The nerve is torn outside the spine. It won't rejoin by itself, but the gap can often be bridged with a nerve graft, usually taken from the lower leg.

    Avulsion

    The root is pulled out of the spinal cord itself. It can't be plugged back in. Surgeons instead reroute a working nerve nearby to power the paralysed muscles (a nerve transfer). A fluid pouch on the scan is a clue to this type.

    Symptoms

    • Weakness or paralysis of the shoulder, elbow, wrist or hand, depending on which roots are injured
    • Numbness or loss of feeling in part of the arm or hand
    • Pain, often burning or crushing, felt in an arm that is also numb. It is especially common after avulsion injuries.
    • A droopy eyelid and small pupil on the same side (Horner's syndrome), which suggests the lowest root has been pulled from the spinal cord

    In a baby, the arm lies still by the side or doesn't move as much as the other arm, and the baby may not use the hand.

    How it is diagnosed

    A careful examination of each muscle and of feeling across the arm shows which roots and nerves are involved. It is repeated over time to look for recovery.

    • X-rays look for broken bones in the neck, collarbone, shoulder and first rib, and check whether the diaphragm muscle on that side is working.
    • MRI or a CT myelogram (a CT scan after dye is injected around the spinal cord) shows whether roots have been pulled from the spinal cord. A small pouch of fluid where a root should be is a typical sign.
    • Nerve conduction studies and EMG test how the nerves and muscles are working. They are most useful about 3 to 4 weeks after the injury, and are repeated to track recovery.

    After a high-energy injury, doctors also check the blood vessels to the arm, which run alongside the nerves.

    Treatment

    • Early care. Pain relief, protecting the arm, and physiotherapy to keep the joints moving freely while the nerves recover. Nerve pain often needs specific medicines, such as gabapentin, pregabalin or amitriptyline, rather than ordinary painkillers.
    • Watching for recovery. Stretch injuries often recover, so the team may examine and test the arm over the first few months.
    • Nerve surgery. Torn nerves can be repaired with a nerve graft, usually taken from the lower leg. When roots are pulled from the spinal cord, a nerve transfer reroutes a working nerve, or part of one, to power a paralysed muscle. Clean cuts are repaired within days. For other injuries, surgery is usually best within about 3 to 6 months, because muscles that go too long without a nerve supply can't recover.
    • Later surgery. If nerve surgery isn't possible or doesn't work, moving a working tendon or muscle to a new position can improve function.
    • Surgery for pain. Rarely, severe pain after avulsion that doesn't respond to medicines is treated with an operation on the spinal cord where the roots were torn out.

    Treatment is usually given by a specialist nerve team, which may include neurosurgeons, plastic or orthopaedic surgeons, physiotherapists and pain specialists.

    In babies

    Most babies with a birth-related brachial plexus injury recover well, often within the first few months. Gentle daily exercises, shown by a physiotherapist, keep the joints supple while the nerves recover. If the elbow still can't bend by about 3 to 6 months, the baby is assessed for nerve surgery.

    Recovery

    Nerves regrow slowly, at about 1 millimetre a day, so recovery is measured in months and can continue for 2 to 3 years. The shoulder and elbow usually recover better than the hand, because the hand is furthest from the injury. Physiotherapy and keeping the joints moving throughout make a real difference to the final result.

    When to seek urgent help

    • A cold, pale or blue arm, or a weak pulse at the wrist, after an injury
    • Weakness or numbness in the arm after a road accident or a fall onto the shoulder
    • Breathlessness or chest pain after the injury

    Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.
