Trigeminal neuralgia

Trigeminal neuralgia causes sudden attacks of severe, sharp, electric-shock-like pain in one side of the face. Many people first see a dentist, because the pain can feel like toothache.

What it is

The trigeminal nerve carries feeling from the face to the brain. In trigeminal neuralgia this nerve sends sudden bursts of pain signals.

The most common cause is a blood vessel pressing on the nerve close to where it joins the brainstem. Less often it is linked to multiple sclerosis or, rarely, a tumour.

Where the pain strikes, and why

Where the pain is felt

Forehead and eye (V1)

The upper branch supplies the forehead, upper eyelid and the bridge of the nose. It is the least commonly affected.

Cheek (V2)

The middle branch supplies the cheek, upper lip, upper teeth and the side of the nose. Pain here is often mistaken for toothache.

Jaw (V3)

The lower branch supplies the lower lip, chin, lower teeth and jaw. Chewing and talking can set off attacks.

The cause

An artery beating against the nerve

Near the brainstem, a small artery loops onto the nerve root. With every heartbeat it knocks against the nerve, wearing away its insulation until signals short-circuit into bursts of pain.

After microvascular decompression

The surgeon lifts the artery away and slips a soft cushion of surgical felt between them. The cushion absorbs each pulse, so the nerve is left in peace.

Symptoms

  • Stabbing or electric-shock pain in the cheek, jaw, teeth, gums, lips or around the eye, usually on one side
  • Attacks lasting from a second to about two minutes, sometimes in rapid clusters
  • Pain triggered by light touch, chewing, talking, brushing teeth, shaving or a breeze
  • Pain-free periods between attacks, which can last weeks or months

How it is diagnosed

The diagnosis is mainly made from the description of the pain. An MRI scan is usually done to look for a blood vessel touching the nerve and to rule out other causes.

Treatment options

  • Medicines first. Certain anti-seizure medicines, such as carbamazepine, calm the nerve and control pain for many people. Ordinary painkillers usually don't help.
  • Microvascular decompression. An operation behind the ear to move the blood vessel away from the nerve and place a small cushion between them. It treats the cause and keeps feeling in the face.
  • Procedures on the nerve itself, using heat, a small balloon, glycerol or focused radiation to reduce pain signals. These are less invasive but may cause some facial numbness, and pain can return.

Surgery or procedures are considered when medicines no longer control the pain or cause side effects that are hard to live with.

When to seek urgent help

  • Facial pain together with new weakness of the face or body
  • Facial pain with loss of vision, a severe headache or confusion

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.

Questions to ask your doctor
