Pituitary tumour

The pituitary is a gland the size of a pea that sits in a small cup of bone beneath the brain and controls many of the body's hormones. Most pituitary tumours (adenomas) are not cancer, and many can be treated very effectively.

What it is

A pituitary adenoma is a slow-growing, benign growth of the gland. Some make too much of a hormone (functioning tumours). Others make no hormone but can grow large enough to press on nearby structures (non-functioning tumours).

Just above the gland, the optic nerves from both eyes cross over. This is why pituitary tumours can affect eyesight.

Treatment

A small tumour

The pituitary gland sits in a cup of bone below the brain, just under the point where the optic nerves cross. Small tumours often only affect hormones, or nothing at all.

Growing upward

A larger tumour grows up out of the cup and presses on the crossing optic nerves from below. The fibres carrying the outer half of vision from each eye cross here, so they are affected first.

Outer vision fades

The outer half of vision in both eyes slowly dims, usually starting at the top. It comes on so gradually that many people only notice when they bump into things. The longer the nerves stay squeezed, the less likely vision is to recover fully.

After surgery

Most tumours are removed through the nose with an endoscope. Vision often improves within days to weeks once the pressure is relieved. If the nerves were squeezed for a long time, recovery may be only partial, shown here as faint shadows that remain, which is why early treatment matters.

Symptoms

From pressure:

  • Gradual loss of the outer edges of vision in both eyes, often first noticed when bumping into things
  • Headaches

From too much hormone, depending on the type:

  • Prolactin: irregular or absent periods, milk from the breasts, low sex drive or erectile problems
  • Growth hormone (acromegaly): growing hands, feet and jaw, coarse facial features, sweating
  • ACTH (Cushing's disease): weight gain around the middle, a rounded face, high blood pressure, diabetes, thin skin

From too little hormone: tiredness, feeling cold, low sex drive and low mood.

How it is diagnosed

An MRI scan focused on the pituitary shows the tumour. Blood tests check every pituitary hormone. A visual field test maps any loss of vision, and is repeated to track it.

Treatment options

  • Tablets. Prolactin-secreting tumours (prolactinomas) usually shrink with tablets such as cabergoline, and often need no surgery.
  • Endoscopic surgery through the nose. Most other tumours that need treatment are removed through the nostril with a thin camera, reaching the gland through the sphenoid sinus. There is no cut on the face, and vision often improves soon after.
  • Radiotherapy for tumour left behind or tumours that return.
  • Monitoring for small tumours that cause no problems.
  • Hormone replacement if the gland no longer makes enough of a hormone.

Will my vision come back?

Vision often improves within days to weeks of surgery, once the pressure on the optic nerves is relieved. But the longer the nerves have been squeezed, the more likely some damage is permanent, and recovery may be only partial. This is why gradual loss of side vision should be checked promptly rather than waited on.

Care is shared between a neurosurgeon and an endocrinologist (a hormone specialist).

The route through the nose

NostrilSinusTumour

Into the nostril

A rigid endoscope, a thin telescope with a light and camera, is passed into the nostril. There is no cut on the face.

Through the nose to the sphenoid sinus

It travels along the natural air passage, between the turbinates, to the sphenoid sinus, an air space at the very back of the nose that lies directly beneath the pituitary.

Opening the floor of the cup

The thin bone forming the roof of the sinus is also the floor of the pituitary's bony cup. A small window is made in it to reach the tumour.

Tumour removed

The tumour is removed piece by piece from below, and the optic nerves settle back down. The window is sealed, sometimes with a little fat or a flap of the nose lining.

When to seek urgent help

  • A sudden, severe headache with vomiting, vision loss or double vision (bleeding into the tumour, called pituitary apoplexy)
  • Rapidly worsening vision
  • Extreme tiredness, vomiting and dizziness in someone with a known pituitary problem

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Is this tumour making any hormones?
  • Is my vision affected, and how will we monitor it?
  • Could tablets treat it, or do I need surgery?
  • Will I need hormone replacement afterwards?
