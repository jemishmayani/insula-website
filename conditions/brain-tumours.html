Brain tumours

A brain tumour is a growth of abnormal cells in the brain or the tissues around it. The word sounds frightening, but brain tumours vary enormously, and many are slow-growing and not cancer.

What it is

Brain tumours are usually described in two ways.

  • Benign or malignant. Benign tumours are not cancer and tend to grow slowly. A common example is a meningioma, which grows from the brain's outer covering. Malignant tumours are cancer and can grow faster.
  • Primary or secondary. A primary tumour starts in the brain. A secondary tumour (also called a metastasis) has spread to the brain from cancer elsewhere in the body.

Even a benign tumour can cause problems, because the skull is a closed space and a growth can press on nearby brain.

How a tumour affects the brain around it

SmallGrowingLarge

Small tumour

Many small tumours cause no symptoms and are found by chance. Some only need to be watched with regular scans.

Growing

As it grows, it can press on nearby brain. The symptoms depend on which area is affected.

Mass effect

A larger tumour and the swelling around it push the brain sideways, shifting the midline. Steroids can reduce the swelling, and surgery may be needed.

Common symptoms

Symptoms depend mostly on where the tumour is, not how big it is. They can include:

  • Headaches that are new, keep getting worse, or are worse in the morning or with vomiting
  • A seizure (fit), especially for the first time as an adult
  • Weakness or numbness on one side of the body
  • Changes in vision, speech, memory, behaviour or personality
  • Problems with balance or coordination

Most headaches are not caused by a tumour. But a new pattern of headache with any of the other symptoms above deserves a medical check.

How it is diagnosed

An MRI scan with contrast dye is the main test. A CT scan is often done first in an emergency. Sometimes a biopsy, taking a small sample of the tumour, is needed to know exactly what type it is. The type guides every treatment decision.

Treatment options

  • Watching and waiting. Small, slow-growing tumours that cause no symptoms are sometimes monitored with regular scans.
  • Surgery. The aim is usually to remove as much of the tumour as can be removed safely, without harming important brain function.
  • Radiotherapy and chemotherapy. These may be used after surgery, or instead of it, depending on the tumour type.
  • Medicines for symptoms. Steroids can reduce swelling around a tumour, and anti-seizure medicines can prevent fits.

Treatment is usually planned by a team that may include a neurosurgeon, oncologist, radiologist and pathologist.

When to seek urgent help

  • A seizure for the first time
  • A sudden, severe headache, or a headache with repeated vomiting and drowsiness
  • Sudden weakness, confusion or loss of vision

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What type of tumour is this likely to be?
  • Do I need a biopsy before deciding on treatment?
  • What are the risks of surgery, and what are the risks of waiting?
  • Who else will be involved in my care?
