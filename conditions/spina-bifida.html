Spina bifida

Spina bifida means the bony arch at the back of one or more vertebrae did not close properly during the first month of pregnancy. It ranges from a hidden gap that causes no problems to an open defect involving the spinal cord, which needs surgery soon after birth.

What it is

  • Spina bifida occulta. A small gap in the bony arch, with normal skin and nerves. It is common and usually harmless.
  • Meningocele. A skin-covered sac of fluid pushes out through the gap, but the spinal cord stays in place.
  • Myelomeningocele. The most serious form. The spinal cord and nerves are exposed in an open sac on the back.

A related group of conditions, closed or occult spinal dysraphism, includes a tethered spinal cord, where the bottom of the cord is stuck down and stretched as the child grows. Clues on the skin include a dimple, tuft of hair, birthmark or fatty lump over the lower spine.

The types of spina bifida

Type

A normal vertebra

The bony arch at the back of each vertebra closes around the spinal cord, covered by muscle and skin. This happens in the first month of pregnancy, often before a woman knows she is pregnant.

Spina bifida occulta

The arch has a small gap, but the cord and skin are normal. It is common, usually harmless and often found by chance. A tuft of hair, a dimple or a birthmark over the spine can sometimes be a clue.

Meningocele

A skin-covered sac of fluid pushes out through the gap. The spinal cord stays in its normal place, so the nerves usually work well. Surgery closes the sac.

Myelomeningocele

The most serious form. The spinal cord and nerves themselves are pushed out into an open sac. It can cause weakness of the legs and problems with bladder and bowel control, and many babies also develop hydrocephalus. The defect is closed by surgery within the first days of life.

After repair

The neurosurgeon places the nerve tissue back into the canal and closes the lining, muscle and skin in layers to protect it and prevent infection. Nerve damage already present does not reverse, so care continues with a team.

Effects

In myelomeningocele, effects depend on the level of the defect and may include:

  • Weakness or paralysis of the legs, and reduced feeling
  • Problems with bladder and bowel control
  • Hydrocephalus, a build-up of fluid in the brain, in many babies, often needing a shunt
  • A Chiari type 2 malformation at the base of the brain
  • Foot and hip problems and a curved spine

How it is diagnosed

Myelomeningocele is usually seen on the anomaly ultrasound scan during pregnancy. After birth, an MRI scan shows the spinal cord in detail. Skin signs over the lower spine in a baby are checked with ultrasound or MRI.

Treatment options

  • Closure of the defect, usually within the first 48 hours of life, to protect the nerves and prevent infection. In a few specialist centres, closure is done before birth.
  • Treatment of hydrocephalus, with a shunt or an endoscopic procedure, if needed.
  • Release of a tethered cord if symptoms develop.
  • Lifelong team care including physiotherapy, urology, orthopaedics and support for learning and independence.

Prevention

Taking folic acid every day from before conception until 12 weeks of pregnancy greatly reduces the risk. Women who have had a previous affected pregnancy, or who take certain anti-seizure medicines, may need a higher dose, so ask your doctor.

When to seek urgent help

  • After a shunt, a child with headache, vomiting, drowsiness or a bulging soft spot
  • New weakness in the legs, or changes in bladder or bowel control
  • Redness, fever or leaking fluid from the repair wound

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.
