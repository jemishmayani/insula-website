    Headache

    Almost everyone has headaches. Most are not dangerous and are caused by the headache itself, such as tension-type headache or migraine. A few are a sign of another problem that needs treatment. Knowing the type, and the warning signs, tells you what to do next.

    What a headache is

    The brain itself can't feel pain. Headache comes from the structures around it: the blood vessels, the coverings of the brain (the meninges), and the nerves, muscles and joints of the head and neck.

    Doctors sort headaches into groups, because the group decides the treatment.

    • Primary headaches. The headache is the condition itself, with no other disease behind it. Tension-type headache, migraine and cluster headache are the main ones. They are by far the most common.
    • Secondary headaches. The headache is a symptom of something else. Usually that is minor, such as a cold, fever, a hangover or too much caffeine. Less often it is a problem that needs urgent treatment, such as bleeding, infection or raised pressure inside the head.
    • Referred headaches. A type of secondary headache in which the pain starts in a nearby part, such as the neck, jaw, teeth, sinuses or eyes, and is felt in the head.

    Choose a type below to see where it usually hurts and how it behaves over time. Real headaches don't always follow the textbook, so use this as a guide, not a diagnosis.

    Tension-type headache

    The most common headache. A pressing or tight band on both sides of the head, mild to moderate, lasting from 30 minutes to several days. It doesn't usually cause vomiting or get worse with walking. Poor sleep, stress, missed meals, too little water and long hours at a screen can bring it on. Simple painkillers, rest, and regular sleep and meals usually help.

    Migraine

    Often one-sided and throbbing, moderate to severe, and worse with movement. It commonly comes with feeling sick or vomiting and a dislike of light and noise, and lasts from 4 hours to 3 days. About 1 in 4 people with migraine have an aura first, such as zigzag lines or flashing lights lasting up to an hour. Painkillers or triptans taken early treat attacks, and preventive tablets help if attacks are frequent.

    Cluster headache

    Rare but extremely severe. The pain is always on one side, in or around the eye, and lasts 15 minutes to 3 hours, up to 8 times a day, often at the same time of day or night. The eye on that side may water and redden, the eyelid may droop, and the nostril may run or block. People tend to pace rather than lie still. Attacks come in bouts lasting weeks. High-flow oxygen and triptan injections or nasal sprays treat attacks.

    Sudden, severe (thunderclap) headache

    A headache that reaches full strength within a minute, often described as the worst ever. Bleeding around the brain, for example from a burst aneurysm, is one cause, and there are other causes involving the blood vessels. It is an emergency: call 112 or go to an emergency department straight away, even if it starts to ease.

    Raised pressure inside the head

    Pressure can rise because of a growth, a blockage of brain fluid (hydrocephalus), or a condition called idiopathic intracranial hypertension. The headache tends to be worse on waking or lying down and with coughing or straining, and gets steadily worse over days or weeks. It may come with vomiting, blurred or double vision, or drowsiness. It needs prompt medical assessment, usually with a scan.

    Painkiller overuse headache

    Taking painkillers for headache too often can itself cause a dull headache on most days. It can happen with simple painkillers on 15 or more days a month, or with triptans, codeine or combination tablets on 10 or more days a month. It usually improves within weeks of cutting down, ideally with a plan from your doctor.

    Sinus headache

    Infection or inflammation of the sinuses causes pressure over the forehead, cheeks or around the eyes, with a blocked nose, thick discharge and sometimes fever. It is often worse when bending forward. Many people who have 'sinus headaches' without these nose symptoms actually have migraine.

    Giant cell arteritis (inflamed temple arteries)

    Inflammation of arteries in the scalp, almost only in people over 50. It causes a new headache, often over the temples, a tender scalp, and jaw pain when chewing. It can cause permanent loss of sight, so it needs medical assessment the same day. Blood tests and steroid tablets started early protect the eyes.

    Headache from the neck (cervicogenic)

    A referred headache: the pain comes from the joints, discs or muscles of the upper neck. It starts in the neck or the back of the head, usually on one side, and spreads forward, sometimes to the forehead or eye. Neck movement or holding the neck in one position brings it on. Physiotherapy, posture changes and treating the neck problem help.

    Headache from the jaw (TMJ)

    A referred headache from the jaw joint, which sits just in front of the ear. Clenching, grinding or a jaw joint problem causes aching at the temples, cheeks and in front of the ear, worse with chewing, often with clicking or a stiff jaw. Tooth problems can cause similar pain. A dentist can help, sometimes with a night guard.

    Primary headaches

    • Tension-type headache. A pressing, tight band on both sides of the head. It is mild to moderate and doesn't usually stop you from doing things. Stress, poor sleep, missed meals and posture are common triggers.
    • Migraine. A moderate to severe, often one-sided, throbbing headache lasting 4 hours to 3 days, with nausea and sensitivity to light and noise. Some people have an aura first, such as zigzag lines in their vision. Migraine often runs in families and is more common in women.
    • Cluster headache. Short, extremely severe attacks around one eye, with a watering eye and a blocked or runny nostril on the same side. Attacks come in bouts, often at the same time each day. It is more common in men and in smokers.
    • Less common primary headaches include brief stabbing headaches, and headaches brought on by coughing, exercise or sex. A first headache of these kinds still needs checking, because the same triggers can occasionally point to another cause.

    Sharp, electric-shock pain in the face is usually a different condition, trigeminal neuralgia.

    Secondary headaches

    Common, usually harmless causes include colds, flu and other infections with fever, dehydration, a hangover, missing your usual caffeine, and some medicines. Taking painkillers too often can itself cause a daily headache (painkiller overuse headache).

    Less common causes need prompt medical care:

    • Bleeding in or around the brain, for example from a burst aneurysm, or after a head injury. Bleeding around the brain often causes a sudden, severe headache.
    • Infection of the brain's coverings (meningitis), with fever, a stiff neck and sometimes a rash.
    • Raised pressure inside the head from a brain tumour, a blockage of brain fluid (hydrocephalus), or idiopathic intracranial hypertension, which mostly affects young women and can threaten vision.
    • Low pressure from a leak of brain fluid, for example after a spinal injection. The headache is worse sitting or standing and eases lying flat.
    • Giant cell arteritis, inflamed arteries in the scalp in people over 50, which can damage sight.
    • Other causes, such as a stroke, very high blood pressure, a Chiari malformation (headache brought on by coughing), and carbon monoxide from a faulty heater or stove (often with others in the house unwell too).

    Referred headaches

    • From the neck (cervicogenic headache). Pain begins in the upper neck or back of the head, usually on one side, and spreads forward. It is linked to neck movement and posture, and to worn joints and discs in the neck.
    • From the jaw. Problems with the jaw joint, or clenching and grinding the teeth, cause aching in the temples and in front of the ear, worse with chewing.
    • From the teeth. A tooth infection or abscess can cause pain spreading into the face and head.
    • From the sinuses. Sinus infection causes pressure over the forehead and cheeks with a blocked nose and discharge.
    • From the eyes. A sudden rise in pressure in the eye (acute glaucoma) causes a painful red eye, blurred vision and vomiting, and is an emergency. Eye strain may cause mild headaches, but is a less common cause than many people think.

    How the cause is found

    The most useful test is a careful history: when the headaches started, how they begin, where they hurt, how long they last, what comes with them, what brings them on, and which medicines you take and how often. A headache diary kept for a few weeks helps a lot.

    The doctor checks your blood pressure, examines the nervous system, and looks at the back of the eyes, where swelling can be a sign of raised pressure.

    Most people with a typical primary headache and a normal examination don't need a scan. A CT or MRI scan is arranged when there are warning signs or the pattern is unusual. For a sudden, severe headache, a CT scan is done urgently, sometimes followed by a lumbar puncture. Blood tests help diagnose giant cell arteritis.

    Treatment

    • Primary headaches. Regular sleep and meals, enough water, regular exercise and managing stress all reduce attacks. Paracetamol, ibuprofen or aspirin treat attacks, and triptans are used for migraine and cluster headache. Preventive tablets help if attacks are frequent. Cluster attacks can be treated with high-flow oxygen.
    • Avoiding painkiller overuse. Use simple painkillers for headache on fewer than 15 days a month, and triptans or combination painkillers on fewer than 10. Codeine and other opioids are best avoided for headache.
    • Secondary headaches. Treatment is aimed at the cause, for example antibiotics for meningitis, steroids for giant cell arteritis, or surgery for a bleed, a tumour or blocked brain fluid.
    • Referred headaches. Physiotherapy and posture changes for the neck, dental care or a night guard for the jaw, and treatment from an eye or ear, nose and throat specialist where needed.

    When to see a neurosurgeon

    Most headaches are looked after by a family doctor, and some by a neurologist. A neurosurgeon is the specialist to see when a headache comes from a problem inside the skull or spine that may need an operation, such as:

    • a brain tumour or cyst
    • a brain aneurysm, or bleeding inside the skull, including a blood clot after a head injury
    • hydrocephalus, or a problem with a shunt
    • a Chiari malformation causing headaches with coughing or straining
    • idiopathic intracranial hypertension when vision is at risk despite other treatment
    • neck problems pressing on nerves or the spinal cord, when they are behind a referred headache

    If your headache has any of the warning signs above, or a scan has shown a problem in the brain or spine, see a neurosurgeon. Take your headache diary, a list of your medicines and any scan reports and images with you.

    When to seek urgent help

    • A sudden, severe headache that reaches full strength within a minute
    • Headache with fever and a stiff neck, a rash, or confusion
    • Headache with weakness, numbness, slurred speech, loss of vision or a seizure
    • Headache after a head injury with vomiting or drowsiness
    • A painful red eye with blurred vision

    Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.
