Carpal tunnel syndrome

Carpal tunnel syndrome is the most common trapped nerve. The median nerve is squeezed as it passes through a narrow tunnel at the wrist, causing tingling, numbness and pain in the hand, typically at night.

What it is

The carpal tunnel is a narrow channel at the front of the wrist. Its floor and walls are wrist bones, and its roof is a tough band called the transverse carpal ligament. Nine tendons that bend the fingers and thumb pass through it, together with the median nerve.

When the contents swell or the space narrows, the nerve is squeezed. It is more common in women, in pregnancy, and in people with diabetes, an underactive thyroid, rheumatoid arthritis or jobs with repetitive wrist movements.

Treatment

A crowded tunnel

Nine tendons that bend the fingers and the median nerve share a narrow tunnel. Bones form the floor and walls, and a tough ligament forms the roof.

Swelling

The linings of the tendons thicken, often with repeated wrist movement, pregnancy, diabetes or an underactive thyroid. Pressure rises in a space that cannot stretch.

The nerve is squeezed

The median nerve flattens. Tingling and numbness spread through the thumb, index, middle and half of the ring finger, often waking people at night. Later the thumb muscles can weaken.

After carpal tunnel release

Through a small cut in the palm, the surgeon divides the ligament forming the roof. The tunnel widens, pressure falls and the nerve can recover. The ligament heals with more room inside.

Symptoms

  • Tingling, numbness or pins and needles in the thumb, index, middle and half of the ring finger
  • Symptoms that wake you at night and ease when you shake or hang the hand
  • Pain that can spread up the forearm
  • Clumsiness and dropping things
  • Later, weakness and wasting of the muscle at the base of the thumb

The little finger is usually not affected, which helps to tell it apart from a trapped nerve in the neck.

How it is diagnosed

The diagnosis is mostly made from the symptoms and an examination. Nerve conduction studies measure how well signals pass through the wrist and show how severe the compression is. An ultrasound scan is sometimes used.

Treatment options

  • A wrist splint at night that keeps the wrist straight often helps mild symptoms.
  • A steroid injection into the tunnel can relieve symptoms, sometimes for months.
  • Carpal tunnel release. A short day-case operation, usually under local anaesthetic, divides the ligament forming the roof of the tunnel to give the nerve more room. Night-time tingling often improves within days. Numbness and weakness that have been present for a long time recover more slowly, and sometimes not completely.

Surgery is advised when symptoms persist despite splinting, or sooner if there is constant numbness or thumb weakness, because these mean the nerve is being damaged.

When to seek urgent help

  • Sudden numbness and weakness of a hand after a wrist injury or fracture
  • Weakness of the arm or face together with hand numbness, which may be a stroke

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.
