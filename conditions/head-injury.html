Head injury

A head injury can range from a mild bump to serious damage inside the skull. Most are mild and get better on their own, but some need urgent care, sometimes hours or even weeks after the injury.

What it is

Concussion is a temporary disturbance of brain function after a blow to the head. It can happen without being knocked out.

More serious injuries include a skull fracture or bleeding inside the skull. A bleed can build up quickly, or, especially in older people and those taking blood thinners, slowly over days to weeks. A slow bleed is called a chronic subdural haematoma.

Where bleeding happens inside the skull

Type of bleed

Normal layers

The brain sits inside a tough lining, the dura, which is firmly attached to the inside of the skull. The falx is a fold of dura that hangs between the two halves of the brain.

Extradural haematoma

A skull fracture tears an artery on the inside of the bone. The blood collects between the skull and the dura, peeling the dura away from the bone into a lens shape. The brain is dented beneath it and pushed across the midline. It can grow quickly, and some people seem well at first, then deteriorate.

Acute subdural haematoma

Veins bridging from the brain to the dura tear. The dura stays against the skull, and blood spreads beneath it over the whole surface of the brain in a crescent. The brain underneath often swells too, so the midline shift is usually greater than the clot alone would suggest.

Chronic subdural haematoma

A slow bleed beneath the dura that builds up over weeks, often in older people whose brain has shrunk slightly with age. Thin membranes form on both sides of the collection. Because it grows slowly, the brain adapts, and symptoms such as confusion or weakness can appear long after a minor fall.

Signs that need a scan

After any head injury, seek emergency care if there is:

  • Any loss of consciousness, or no memory of the injury
  • Repeated vomiting
  • A headache that keeps getting worse
  • Drowsiness, confusion or unusual behaviour
  • A seizure
  • Clear fluid or blood from the nose or ears
  • Weakness, numbness or difficulty speaking
  • The person takes blood-thinning medicines

How it is diagnosed

A CT scan is the main test, because it quickly shows bleeding and fractures. Doctors also check alertness, the pupils and limb strength, often repeatedly over several hours.

Treatment options

  • Observation. Many people are watched in hospital for a period, because some problems only appear after a few hours.
  • Surgery. A large blood clot pressing on the brain may need to be removed through an opening in the skull. A chronic subdural haematoma can often be drained through small holes.
  • Intensive care for severe injuries, to control pressure inside the skull and support breathing.

Recovering from concussion

After a short period of rest, a gradual return to normal activity is usually advised. Headaches, tiredness, poor concentration and irritability can last for weeks. Avoid contact sports until a doctor has cleared you, because a second injury before recovery can be more serious.

When to seek urgent help

  • Loss of consciousness, repeated vomiting or a worsening headache
  • Increasing drowsiness, confusion or a seizure
  • An older person on blood thinners who becomes confused days or weeks after a fall

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.

  • When is it safe to return to work, driving or sport?
