Brain aneurysm

A brain aneurysm is a bulge in a weak part of a blood vessel wall, a little like a weak spot on a tyre. Most aneurysms never burst, and many are found by chance on a scan done for another reason.

What it is

Blood vessels in the brain are under constant pressure. Where the wall is weaker, it can slowly balloon outward. This bulge is an aneurysm.

If an aneurysm bursts (ruptures), blood leaks into the space around the brain. This is called a subarachnoid haemorrhage and is a medical emergency.

An aneurysm: untreated, ruptured, coiled or clipped

Show

Untreated

Blood pushes into the thin-walled dome with every heartbeat. Many small aneurysms never burst and are simply monitored.

Ruptured

If the dome tears, blood escapes into the space around the brain, a subarachnoid haemorrhage. It causes a sudden, severe headache and is an emergency.

Coiling

A thin tube is steered up through the arteries into the aneurysm, and soft platinum coils fill the dome from inside. Blood stops entering it, and no opening in the skull is needed.

Clipping

During an operation, a small spring-loaded metal clip closes across the neck of the aneurysm. The dome is sealed off while blood keeps flowing normally through the parent artery.

Symptoms

An aneurysm that has not burst usually causes no symptoms at all. Occasionally a larger one presses on a nerve, which can cause a drooping eyelid, double vision, or pain above or behind one eye.

A burst aneurysm typically causes a sudden, extremely severe headache, often described as the worst headache of one's life, reaching full strength within seconds. It may come with vomiting, a stiff neck, sensitivity to light, confusion or collapse.

Who is more at risk

  • Smoking
  • High blood pressure
  • A close family member who has had an aneurysm
  • Some inherited conditions, such as polycystic kidney disease

Stopping smoking and controlling blood pressure are the most useful things most people can do.

How it is diagnosed

Aneurysms are found with scans that show blood vessels: CT angiography, MR angiography, or a catheter angiogram, where dye is injected through a thin tube passed from the wrist or groin.

Treatment options

  • Monitoring. Many small aneurysms are safely watched with repeat scans, alongside blood pressure control and stopping smoking.
  • Coiling. A thin tube is passed through a blood vessel up to the aneurysm, which is filled with soft metal coils so blood no longer flows into it. No opening in the skull is needed.
  • Clipping. In an operation, a small metal clip is placed across the neck of the aneurysm to seal it off.

The right choice depends on the aneurysm's size, shape and position, and on your age and general health. For some people, monitoring is safer than any procedure.

When to seek urgent help

  • A sudden, severe headache that peaks within seconds or a minute
  • Headache with vomiting, a stiff neck or confusion
  • Collapse or loss of consciousness

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • How large is the aneurysm, and where is it?
  • What is the risk of treating it compared with watching it?
  • Is coiling or clipping more suitable for me, and why?
  • Should my family members be screened?
