Craniosynostosis

A baby's skull is made of several bony plates joined by flexible seams called sutures. In craniosynostosis, one or more sutures fuse too early. The skull cannot grow normally at that seam, so it grows in other directions and takes on a characteristic shape.

What it is

The sutures stay open in infancy so the skull can expand as the brain grows rapidly. When a suture fuses early, growth across it stops and the skull compensates by growing more elsewhere. The type is named after the suture involved: sagittal (the most common), metopic, coronal or, rarely, lambdoid.

Most cases happen on their own. Some, especially when several sutures fuse, are part of a genetic syndrome.

A much more common cause of a flat head is positional plagiocephaly, flattening from lying in the same position. It is not craniosynostosis, does not need surgery, and usually improves with repositioning and tummy time.

How an early-fusing suture shapes the skull

Suture fused early

Open sutures

A baby's skull is made of separate plates joined by flexible seams called sutures, with a soft spot (fontanelle) where they meet. They let the skull expand as the brain grows quickly in the first years.

Sagittal synostosis

The seam along the top of the head fuses. The skull can't grow wider, so it grows longer instead, giving a long, narrow head. This is the most common type.

Metopic synostosis

The seam running down the forehead fuses. The forehead becomes narrow and pointed like the prow of a boat, with a ridge down the middle and eyes set close together.

Coronal synostosis (one side)

One of the seams running across the top of the head fuses. The forehead and brow on that side flatten, the other side bulges forward, and the nose can drift towards one side.

Signs

  • An unusual head shape noticed at birth or in the first months, such as a long narrow head, a pointed forehead, or a forehead flattened on one side
  • A hard ridge along a suture
  • A soft spot (fontanelle) that closes early
  • Rarely, signs of raised pressure: irritability, vomiting or poor feeding

How it is diagnosed

An experienced doctor can usually tell from the head shape. A low-dose CT scan with 3D images confirms which sutures are fused and helps plan surgery.

Treatment options

  • Endoscopic strip craniectomy. In young babies, usually under about 4 months, the fused suture can be released through small incisions, followed by a moulding helmet for several months.
  • Cranial vault remodelling. In older babies, the skull bones are reshaped and repositioned in an open operation, usually between 6 and 12 months of age.
  • Monitoring for very mild single-suture cases.

Surgery gives room for the growing brain and improves the head shape. Care is by a team including a paediatric neurosurgeon and a craniofacial surgeon.

When to seek urgent help

  • A baby with persistent vomiting, unusual sleepiness or irritability
  • A bulging soft spot, or a head that is growing very fast

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.
