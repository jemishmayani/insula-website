Slipped disc and sciatica

A 'slipped disc' is a common cause of back and leg pain. Despite the name, nothing actually slips out of place, and most people get better without an operation.

  3. How it is diagnosed

  5. When to seek urgent help

What it is

Between the bones of the spine are discs: tough outer rings with a soft, jelly-like centre. If the outer ring weakens, the centre can push out. Doctors call this a disc prolapse or herniation.

If the bulge presses on a nearby nerve, it causes pain along that nerve's path. When this affects the lower back, the pain runs down the leg and is called sciatica. In the neck, it causes arm pain.

HealthyBulgeOn the nerve

Healthy disc

The soft centre sits safely inside its tough outer ring. The nerve beside it has plenty of room.

A bulge

The centre pushes the outer ring outward. Bulges like this are common on scans and often cause no pain at all.

Pressing on the nerve

Now the bulge touches the nerve. Pain, tingling or numbness travels along it into the leg. This is sciatica, and most people improve without surgery.

Symptoms

  • Leg pain that is often worse than the back pain, sometimes reaching the foot
  • Numbness, tingling or pins and needles in the leg or foot
  • Weakness in the leg or foot
  • Pain that is worse when sitting, bending, coughing or sneezing

How it is diagnosed

A doctor can often make the diagnosis from your symptoms and an examination. An MRI scan is usually done if symptoms are severe, aren't improving after several weeks, or if surgery is being considered.

Disc bulges are very common on scans of people with no pain at all, so the scan is only part of the picture.

Treatment options

  • Staying active. Gentle movement and walking usually help more than bed rest.
  • Pain relief and physiotherapy to manage symptoms while the disc settles.
  • Nerve root injections can reduce severe pain for some people.
  • Surgery. A microdiscectomy removes the part of the disc pressing on the nerve, through a small opening. It may be advised if severe pain doesn't improve, if weakness is getting worse, or in an emergency such as cauda equina syndrome.

Most people improve within a few weeks to a few months.

When to seek urgent help

  • Numbness around the groin, buttocks or inner thighs
  • New difficulty passing urine, or loss of bladder or bowel control
  • Weakness in both legs, or a foot that is quickly getting weaker

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.
