Stroke

A stroke happens when the blood supply to part of the brain is suddenly cut off, either by a clot blocking an artery or by an artery bursting. It is a medical emergency, and the faster treatment starts, the more of the brain can be saved.

What it is

There are two main types.

  • Ischaemic stroke (about four in five strokes). A clot blocks an artery, and the brain beyond it is starved of oxygen. The centre of the affected area is damaged quickly, but the area around it can often be saved for a few hours if blood flow returns.
  • Haemorrhagic stroke. An artery bursts and bleeds into or around the brain. Bleeding deep inside the brain is often linked to long-standing high blood pressure. Bleeding around the brain is often from a burst aneurysm.

A transient ischaemic attack (TIA), or mini-stroke, causes the same symptoms but they go away within minutes to hours. It is a warning sign that a full stroke may follow, and it needs urgent assessment too.

A stroke: a blocked artery or a bleed

Show

Blood flowing normally

Each carotid artery divides into the anterior cerebral artery, which runs up between the two halves of the brain, and the middle cerebral artery, which runs out through the Sylvian fissure to the side of the brain. Tiny deep arteries branch off it to feed the deep movement pathways.

A clot blocks the artery

A clot lodges in the middle cerebral artery. Everything beyond it, deep and on the surface, is starved of blood, while the anterior cerebral artery keeps flowing. The centre dies first; the area around it can still be saved for a few hours. Watch the clock: every minute counts.

Clot removed

Clot-busting medicine, or a thin tube steered up from the groin or wrist to pull the clot out, restores the blood flow. What has already died cannot recover, but the brain at risk is saved. The sooner, the better.

A bleed

One of the tiny deep arteries, weakened by years of high blood pressure, bursts. The clot and the swelling around it squash the ventricle and push the brain across the midline. Treatment controls blood pressure and swelling, and a neurosurgeon may remove the clot or relieve pressure in some cases.

Recognising a stroke: think FAST

  • Face: has one side of the face drooped? Can they smile evenly?
  • Arms: can they lift both arms and keep them there?
  • Speech: is their speech slurred, muddled, or have they lost it?
  • Time: call 112 immediately, and note the time the symptoms started.

Other sudden symptoms include loss of vision, double vision, severe dizziness with unsteadiness, confusion, or a sudden severe headache.

How it is diagnosed

An urgent CT scan of the brain shows whether there is bleeding, which decides the treatment. CT angiography shows whether a large artery is blocked. An MRI scan, heart tests and blood tests help find the cause.

Treatment options

  • Clot-busting medicine (thrombolysis) can dissolve the clot if given within a few hours of the start of symptoms, in suitable patients.
  • Clot retrieval (thrombectomy). A thin tube is passed from the groin or wrist up to the blocked artery, and the clot is pulled out. It works best for large blocked arteries and the earlier it is done, the better.
  • For bleeding, blood pressure is lowered carefully and any blood thinners are reversed. A neurosurgeon may remove a large clot, drain fluid, or, for severe brain swelling, temporarily remove part of the skull to relieve pressure.
  • Preventing another stroke with blood pressure control, medicines to thin the blood or lower cholesterol, treating an irregular heartbeat, and stopping smoking.
  • Rehabilitation with physiotherapy, speech therapy and occupational therapy helps the brain relearn lost skills.

When to seek urgent help

  • Face drooping, arm weakness or speech difficulty, even if it gets better
  • Sudden loss of vision, severe dizziness or loss of balance
  • A sudden, severe headache unlike any before

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What type of stroke was it, and what caused it?
  • Was the person suitable for clot-busting treatment or clot retrieval?
  • What can we do to prevent another stroke?
  • What rehabilitation will help, and for how long?
