Lumbar canal stenosis

Lumbar canal stenosis means the space for the nerves in the lower back has become narrower, usually because of age-related wear. It typically causes pain or heaviness in the legs when walking or standing.

What it is

Over the years, discs can bulge, ligaments can thicken and small bony growths can form around the spine's joints. Together, these can squeeze the nerves running down the spinal canal. Stenosis simply means narrowing.

What narrowing does to the nerves

NoneModerateSevere

Plenty of room

The nerves sit comfortably in the spinal canal, surrounded by bone in front, at the sides and behind.

Narrowing

The disc bulges back, the facet joints enlarge, and the ligamentum flavum behind the canal thickens. Together they slowly crowd the space, usually with age.

Nerves squeezed

The canal becomes a narrow clover shape. The nerves are squeezed, causing pain, heaviness or numbness in the legs on standing and walking.

Symptoms

  • Pain, heaviness, numbness or weakness in the buttocks and legs that comes on with walking or standing
  • Relief from sitting down or bending forward. Many people notice they can walk further leaning on a shopping trolley
  • A walking distance that gradually gets shorter

Why standing hurts and bending forward helps

Bending forwardNeutralStanding straight

Bending forward

Bending forward stretches the ligament behind the canal flat and pulls the spinal bones apart at the back. The canal opens up, giving the nerves more room.

Neutral

In a neutral position the canal is moderately narrow.

Standing straight

Standing straight or leaning back folds the ligament inward and pushes the disc backwards, narrowing the canal. That's why walking upright hurts, and sitting or leaning on a shopping trolley brings relief.

How it is diagnosed

An MRI scan shows the narrowing. Doctors may also check the circulation in your legs, because poor blood flow can cause similar leg pain on walking.

Treatment options

  • Non-surgical care. Physiotherapy, exercises such as cycling that keep the spine slightly bent forward, pain relief and sometimes injections.
  • Decompression surgery. An operation, often called a laminectomy, removes the bone and thickened ligament pressing on the nerves to make more space.
  • Fusion. If the spine is unstable, the surgeon may also join two bones together using screws and rods.

Surgery is usually considered when symptoms seriously limit daily life despite non-surgical treatment.

When to seek urgent help

  • Numbness around the groin or new bladder or bowel problems
  • Rapidly worsening weakness in the legs

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • How much of my leg pain is coming from the spine?
  • What can I expect if I choose not to have surgery?
  • Do I need a fusion, or decompression alone?
