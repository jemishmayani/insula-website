Cervical myelopathy

Cervical myelopathy happens when the spinal cord is squeezed in the neck. It often develops slowly and can be mistaken for normal ageing, so it is easy to miss.

  3. How it is diagnosed

What it is

The spinal cord runs through a channel in the neck bones. Wear and tear, bulging discs, thickened ligaments or bony growths can narrow this channel and press on the cord. In some people a ligament behind the spine gradually turns to bone, a condition called OPLL.

This differs from a pinched nerve root in the neck (radiculopathy), which mainly causes arm pain.

When discs press on the spinal cord

NoneModerateSevere

Surgery

Room around the cord

The spinal cord runs freely down its channel, carrying signals to the arms and legs.

Narrowing

Bulging discs in front and thickened ligaments behind begin to press on the cord.

Cord compressed

Signals pass less well, causing clumsy hands, numbness and unsteady walking. This usually worsens slowly over time.

After decompression surgery

The discs pressing on the cord are removed and the bones fixed with a small plate, giving the cord room again. The main aim is to stop further damage.

Symptoms

  • Clumsy hands: difficulty with buttons, writing, or picking up small objects
  • Dropping things
  • Unsteady walking or poor balance
  • Numbness or tingling in the hands
  • Sometimes changes in bladder control

Neck pain may be mild or absent.

How it is diagnosed

An examination often shows brisk reflexes and other signs of spinal cord pressure. An MRI scan confirms where and how much the cord is compressed.

Treatment options

Myelopathy usually progresses over time. The main aim of surgery is to stop it getting worse. How much lost function returns varies from person to person, which is why timing matters.

  • Surgery from the front of the neck, such as removing the disc and fusing the bones (ACDF).
  • Surgery from the back of the neck, such as widening the canal (laminoplasty) or a laminectomy with fusion.
  • Careful monitoring may be reasonable in very mild cases.

Avoid neck manipulation if you have been told your spinal cord is compressed.

When to seek urgent help

  • Sudden weakness in the arms or legs after a fall or neck injury
  • Rapidly worsening walking or new loss of bladder control

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • How badly is the spinal cord compressed?
  • Is my condition getting worse, and how will we know?
  • Would surgery from the front or back suit me, and why?
  • What improvement can I realistically expect?
