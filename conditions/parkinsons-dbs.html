Parkinson's disease and DBS

Parkinson's disease is a slowly progressive condition of the brain that affects movement. It is treated mainly with medicines, but for some people a neurosurgical treatment called deep brain stimulation (DBS) can make a big difference.

What it is

Deep in the brain, a group of cells called the substantia nigra makes dopamine, a chemical messenger that keeps movement smooth. In Parkinson's disease these cells slowly die. Movement circuits then fire in an abnormal rhythm.

Parkinson's tremor and deep brain stimulation

Stimulator

Stimulator off

In Parkinson's disease, the dopamine cells of the substantia nigra slowly die. Movement circuits deep in the brain then fire in an abnormal rhythm, causing tremor, stiffness and slowness. The tremor is typically a few beats a second, at rest.

Stimulator on

A fine electrode in the subthalamic nucleus delivers tiny, rapid electrical pulses from a battery under the collarbone, like a pacemaker for the brain. The abnormal rhythm is interrupted and the tremor settles. It does not cure Parkinson's, but can greatly improve movement and reduce the need for medicines.

Symptoms

  • A tremor, typically at rest, often starting in one hand
  • Stiffness of the muscles
  • Slowness of movement, with small handwriting, a quieter voice and less facial expression
  • A shuffling walk and problems with balance later on
  • Non-movement symptoms such as loss of smell, constipation, acting out dreams, low mood and sleep problems, which can begin years earlier

How it is diagnosed

The diagnosis is made by a neurologist from the history and examination. Scans are mainly used to rule out other causes. A good response to Parkinson's medicine supports the diagnosis.

Treatment options

  • Medicines such as levodopa replace or mimic dopamine and work well for many years for most people.
  • Exercise, physiotherapy and speech therapy help keep movement and voice strong.
  • Deep brain stimulation (DBS). Fine electrodes are placed in a precise target deep in the brain, usually the subthalamic nucleus, and connected to a battery under the skin of the chest. Tiny electrical pulses interrupt the abnormal rhythm.

DBS does not cure Parkinson's or stop it progressing. It is usually considered when medicines still work but cause troublesome fluctuations or involuntary movements, or when tremor cannot be controlled. It can reduce tremor, stiffness and slowness and lower the dose of medicines. Careful assessment by a team is needed to choose suitable patients.

When to seek urgent help

  • Sudden confusion, high fever and severe stiffness, especially after stopping or missing Parkinson's medicines
  • A fall with a head injury, especially if taking blood thinners
  • Fever, redness or discharge around a DBS wound or battery

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.
