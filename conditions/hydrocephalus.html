Hydrocephalus

Hydrocephalus means a build-up of fluid inside the brain. The brain normally makes and drains a clear fluid all the time. When drainage is blocked or slowed, fluid collects and pressure rises.

What it is

The fluid is called cerebrospinal fluid, or CSF. It cushions the brain and spinal cord and flows through spaces inside the brain called ventricles.

Hydrocephalus can be present from birth, or develop later after a brain bleed, infection, tumour or head injury. In older adults, a slower form called normal pressure hydrocephalus can develop without an obvious cause.

How fluid flows, and what happens when it's blocked

Show

Normal flow

Fluid made inside the ventricles flows through a narrow channel, the aqueduct, then out around the brain and spinal cord, and is absorbed into a large vein at the top.

Aqueduct blocked

When the aqueduct is narrowed or blocked, fluid can't escape. The ventricles above the blockage swell, stretching the brain and raising pressure.

With a shunt

A thin tube drains fluid from the ventricle under the skin to the abdomen. A valve behind the ear controls how much flows.

Endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV)

Using a thin camera, the surgeon makes a small opening in the floor of the third ventricle, so fluid bypasses the blockage. No tube is left behind.

Symptoms

In babies: a head that grows unusually fast, a bulging soft spot on the head, vomiting, sleepiness, irritability, or eyes that seem fixed downward.

In older children and adults: headache, nausea or vomiting, blurred vision, drowsiness and confusion.

Normal pressure hydrocephalus classically causes three things together: difficulty walking, often with a shuffling gait; trouble controlling the bladder; and memory or thinking problems. It is sometimes mistaken for ageing or dementia, which matters because it can often be treated.

How it is diagnosed

A CT or MRI scan shows enlarged fluid spaces. In babies, an ultrasound through the soft spot can be used. For normal pressure hydrocephalus, doctors may remove some fluid with a needle in the lower back to see whether walking improves.

Treatment options

  • A shunt. A thin, soft tube carries excess fluid from the brain to another part of the body, usually the abdomen, where it is absorbed naturally. A valve controls how much fluid drains.
  • Endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV). Using a thin camera, the surgeon makes a small opening inside the brain so fluid can bypass the blockage. This avoids leaving a tube in the body, but is suitable only for certain types of blockage.
  • Treating the cause, such as removing a tumour that blocks fluid flow.

Living with a shunt

Most people with a shunt lead full lives. Shunts can occasionally block or become infected, so it helps to know the warning signs below and to keep a record of your shunt type and the hospital that placed it.

When to seek urgent help

  • Someone with a shunt develops headache, vomiting and drowsiness
  • Fever, or redness and swelling along the shunt
  • A baby with a fast-growing head, a bulging soft spot or unusual sleepiness

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.
