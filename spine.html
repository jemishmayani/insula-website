Spine

Most back and neck pain gets better without an operation. Spine surgery is considered when there is pressure on the nerves or spinal cord that isn't improving, or is causing weakness.

Spine conditions

Common problems affecting the neck and back.

Slipped disc and sciatica

A disc pressing on a nerve, often causing leg or arm pain. Most people improve without surgery.

Read the guide

Lumbar canal stenosis

Narrowing in the lower back that causes leg pain and heaviness on walking.

Read the guide

Cervical myelopathy

Pressure on the spinal cord in the neck, causing clumsy hands and unsteady walking.

Read the guide

Cauda equina syndrome

An emergency where the nerves controlling the bladder and bowel are squeezed.

Read the guide

Spinal tuberculosis

A TB infection of the spine, usually treated with medicines, and sometimes surgery.

Read the guide

Other conditions

Including spondylolisthesis (one bone slipping on another), spinal tumours and spinal injuries.

Why your back can hurt your leg

Drag the slider to see what happens when a disc in the lower back starts to bulge.

HealthyBulgeOn the nerve

Healthy disc

The soft centre sits safely inside its tough outer ring. The nerve beside it has plenty of room.

A bulge

The centre pushes the outer ring outward. Bulges like this are common on scans and often cause no pain at all.

Pressing on the nerve

Now the bulge touches the nerve. Pain, tingling or numbness travels along it into the leg. This is sciatica, and most people improve without surgery.

Treatment options

Treatment usually begins with the least invasive option that is likely to work.

Without surgery

  • Staying active. Gentle movement usually helps more than bed rest.
  • Physiotherapy. Exercises to improve strength, flexibility and posture.
  • Medicines. To ease pain and nerve symptoms while the problem settles.
  • Injections. Targeted injections can reduce nerve pain for some people.

With surgery

  • Microdiscectomy. Removing the part of a disc pressing on a nerve, through a small opening.
  • Decompression. Removing bone or ligament to create more space for the nerves, often called a laminectomy.
  • Fusion. Joining two or more bones with screws and rods when the spine is unstable.
  • Neck surgery. Operations from the front or back of the neck to relieve pressure on the spinal cord or nerves.

When is surgery considered?

  • Severe nerve pain that hasn't improved after a reasonable period of non-surgical treatment
  • Weakness or numbness that is getting worse
  • Pressure on the spinal cord causing problems with hand function or walking
  • An emergency, such as cauda equina syndrome
  • Instability, infection or a tumour affecting the spine

Even then, the decision is yours. A good discussion covers what surgery can and cannot achieve, the risks, and what is likely to happen without it.

  1. 01

    Consultation

    A conversation about your symptoms, history and concerns, followed by an examination.

  2. 02

    Tests and scans

    Usually an MRI or CT scan, and sometimes other tests, to understand the problem clearly.

  3. 03

    Discussing options

    Every reasonable option is explained, including not operating, with its likely benefits and risks.

  4. 04

    Deciding together

    You take time to think, ask questions, and seek a second opinion if you wish.

  5. 05

    Treatment and recovery

    If you choose surgery, you receive clear guidance on preparation, the hospital stay and recovery.

  6. 06

    Follow-up

    Regular reviews to check progress and adjust plans if needed.

Spine warning signs

  • Numbness around the groin, buttocks or inner thighs
  • New difficulty passing urine, or loss of bladder or bowel control
  • Weakness in both legs, or weakness that is quickly getting worse

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.
