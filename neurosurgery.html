Neurosurgery

Neurosurgery is the surgical care of the brain, skull, spinal cord and nerves. It also includes the many conditions that are best treated without an operation.

Areas of care

The main conditions neurosurgeons assess and treat.

Brain tumours

Growths in or around the brain, both cancerous and non-cancerous. Treatment may include surgery, monitoring, radiotherapy or chemotherapy.

Read the guide

Aneurysms and brain bleeds

Weak, bulging blood vessels and bleeding inside the skull. Some need treatment; many small aneurysms only need monitoring.

Read the guide

Hydrocephalus

A build-up of fluid inside the brain, treated with a shunt or an endoscopic procedure.

Read the guide

Head injury

From concussion to bleeding inside the skull. Some injuries need observation, others urgent surgery.

Read the guide

Trigeminal neuralgia

Severe, electric-shock-like facial pain, treated with medicines or, if needed, surgery.

Read the guide

Stroke

A blocked or burst blood vessel in the brain. Some strokes need a neurosurgeon to remove a clot or relieve pressure.

Read the guide

Pituitary tumours

Usually benign growths of the hormone gland, often removed through the nose with an endoscope.

Read the guide

Neurocysticercosis

Tapeworm cysts in the brain, a common cause of seizures in India, usually treated with medicines.

Read the guide

Chiari malformation

The lowest part of the brain sitting too low, treated with decompression surgery when needed.

Read the guide

Parkinson's disease and DBS

Deep brain stimulation for tremor and movement problems that medicines alone can't control.

Read the guide

Children's neurosurgery

Including craniosynostosis, spina bifida and hydrocephalus in babies and children.

Read the guide

Types of treatment

Surgery is one tool among several. The right approach depends on the condition, its severity and what matters to you.

Monitoring

Some conditions are safest watched with regular scans rather than treated straight away.

Medicines

To control symptoms such as swelling, seizures or nerve pain.

Microsurgery

Delicate surgery using an operating microscope to see and protect fine structures.

Endoscopic surgery

Using a thin camera through a small opening, for example through the nose for some pituitary tumours.

Shunts and drains

Thin tubes that drain excess fluid from the brain.

Team care

Working with oncologists, radiologists and interventional specialists, for example for aneurysm coiling.

What to expect

The usual path from first appointment to follow-up.

  1. 01

    Consultation

    A conversation about your symptoms, history and concerns, followed by an examination.

  2. 02

    Tests and scans

    Usually an MRI or CT scan, and sometimes other tests, to understand the problem clearly.

  3. 03

    Discussing options

    Every reasonable option is explained, including not operating, with its likely benefits and risks.

  4. 04

    Deciding together

    You take time to think, ask questions, and seek a second opinion if you wish.

  5. 05

    Treatment and recovery

    If you choose surgery, you receive clear guidance on preparation, the hospital stay and recovery.

  6. 06

    Follow-up

    Regular reviews to check progress and adjust plans if needed.

Brain warning signs

  • A sudden, severe headache that peaks within seconds
  • Sudden weakness, numbness or drooping on one side
  • Sudden difficulty speaking, or loss of vision
  • A first seizure

Call 112 or go to your nearest emergency department. Don't wait for a clinic appointment or a reply to an email.
